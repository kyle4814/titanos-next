AI Receptionist and the Privacy Act: A Checklist

← Back to the archive
logged 28 November 2026 · 9 min read

People ask whether an AI receptionist is compliant with the Privacy Act as if the answer lives inside the product. It does not. Compliance sits with the business that uses the tool. A vendor can make your job easier or harder, but you are the one who answers to the customer and, if the Act applies to you, to the regulator. This guide sets out who is covered, which privacy principles bite when an AI answers your phone, and the questions to put to a vendor before you sign anything.

We wrote a shorter version of this earlier. If you want the three-question summary, read Is an AI receptionist Privacy Act compliant? first, then come back here for the working.

Step one: does the Privacy Act apply to you at all?

The Privacy Act 1988 (Cth) binds Australian Government agencies and most private businesses with an annual turnover of more than $3 million. Smaller businesses are generally exempt, with important exceptions. A small business is still covered if it provides a health service and holds health information, if it trades in personal information (buying or selling it for a benefit), if it is a contracted service provider for a Commonwealth contract, or if it is a related company of a covered business, among other cases. Credit reporting and tax file number rules apply regardless of size.

The $3 million figure is annual turnover, not profit. The reform process has proposed removing the small business exemption, but at the time of writing it has not been removed, so check the current position before you rely on it. Even exempt businesses often choose to follow the principles, because customers, insurers and larger clients expect it.

What an AI receptionist actually collects

An AI phone assistant does more than a message bank. Depending on the product it may capture:

  • the caller's phone number and the time of the call;
  • the caller's name, address and what they said about the job;
  • the audio of the call and a written transcript of it;
  • a summary or a classification such as urgent or quote request;
  • sometimes payment details or health information the caller volunteers.

Each of those is personal information if it identifies, or could reasonably identify, a person. A voice recording is personal information. A transcript is too. Once you collect them, they belong in your privacy practices, not just the vendor's.

The privacy principles that matter most

The Privacy Act contains 13 Australian Privacy Principles (APPs). Six of them do most of the work for an AI receptionist.

APP 1: open and transparent management

A covered business must have a clear, current privacy policy that says what personal information it collects, why, and whether it is likely to be disclosed overseas. If you add an AI receptionist, update the policy so it mentions the tool and what it captures.

APP 3 and APP 5: collection and notice

You should collect only what you need, by lawful and fair means. At or before the time of collection, or as soon as practicable afterwards, you must take reasonable steps to tell the person who you are, why you are collecting the information, who you might disclose it to and how to find your privacy policy. On a phone call, that means a short spoken notice at the start. It also lines up with the recording consent rules covered in our call recording guide.

Recording has its own state and federal rules. See call recording consent in Australia.

APP 6: use and disclosure

Information collected to book a job should be used to book that job. If the vendor uses recordings to train its own models, ask whether that is a use you are authorising and whether the callers have been told.

APP 8: cross-border disclosure

Many AI phone products send audio or transcripts to servers overseas, and some pass text to a language model provider in another country. Before disclosing personal information to an overseas recipient, you must take reasonable steps to make sure it does not breach the APPs. Under section 16C of the Act, you can also remain accountable for what the overseas recipient does with it. This is the principle most small businesses have never considered.

APP 11: security, retention and destruction

You must take reasonable steps to protect personal information from misuse, interference, loss and unauthorised access, and to destroy or de-identify it once you no longer need it. Ask how long recordings are kept and whether you can delete them.

APP 12 and APP 13: access and correction

A person can ask to see the information you hold about them and to correct it. If recordings and transcripts sit in a vendor system, can you find and give them a copy? Can you delete them on request?

A checklist to put to the vendor

Send these questions by email and keep the answers. A vendor who gives vague replies has given you an answer.

  1. Where are call recordings and transcripts stored, in which country, and with which hosting provider?
  2. Which third parties see the audio or text, such as speech-to-text or language model providers, and where are they based?
  3. Is any customer data used to train or improve your models or anyone else's? Can we switch that off?
  4. How long do you keep recordings and transcripts, and can we set the period or delete on request?
  5. Does the system play a disclosure at the start of the call, and can we edit the wording?
  6. How does the system handle sensitive information, such as health details or card numbers a caller reads out?
  7. What happens if you suffer a data breach? Will you tell us in writing and within what time?
  8. Do your terms include a data processing clause that binds you to our instructions? Please send a copy.
  9. Can we export or delete all data if we cancel?
  10. Is there a human handover, and does it avoid an automated decision about the caller being final?

What to tell your callers

A short spoken line at the start covers the basics. Something like this is a fair starting point, to adapt with your lawyer if the stakes are high:

Thanks for calling Coastal Electrical. You have reached an automated assistant. We record and transcribe this call to take down your details and arrange a callback. Our privacy policy is at our website. If you would rather speak to a person, say so now.

It tells the caller that it is automated, that it is recorded, why, and that there is a way out. None of that is awkward, and it is the sort of transparency the Act is aiming for.

Automated decisions and the December 2026 change

The Privacy and Other Legislation Amendment Act 2024 added a transparency requirement for automated decision-making. From 10 December 2026, a covered business whose privacy policy relates to decisions that use a computer program to make or help make a decision that could significantly affect a person's rights or interests must say so in its policy. A receptionist that books callbacks is unlikely to make that kind of decision. A system that decides who gets a service, a price or a refund might. If you use AI to triage or reject enquiries, read the OAIC guidance and our note on the wording.

If you are covered and need wording help, our Privacy ADM Wording Guide sets out examples in plain English.

Do you need to stop using AI?

No. The point of the checklist is to use the tool with your eyes open. The Office of the Australian Information Commissioner (OAIC) has published guidance on privacy and the use of commercially available AI products. Its message is that the Act applies to AI the same way it applies to any other tool, so businesses should do due diligence, be transparent, and avoid putting personal information into a tool unless they understand what happens to it.

A balanced approach for a small trade business looks like this: pick a vendor that stores data in Australia or can explain exactly where it goes, give callers a short notice, keep retention short, and review the setup once a year.
