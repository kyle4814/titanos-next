Privacy Policy Wording for Automated Decisions

← Back to the archive
logged 28 November 2026 · 9 min read

A new line in the Privacy Act starts to bite on 10 December 2026. Businesses covered by the Act that use automated decision-making will need to say so in their privacy policy. Most law firm commentary explains the rule. Very little of it shows you what the sentence actually looks like. This guide does both: who is affected, what the rule asks for, and example wording you can adapt for a small Australian business.

This is general information, not legal advice. The wording below is a starting point. Have a lawyer review the final version if automated decisions matter to your business.

What changes on 10 December 2026

The Privacy and Other Legislation Amendment Act 2024 amended Australian Privacy Principle 1, which covers open and transparent management of personal information. From 10 December 2026, if a covered entity has arranged for a computer program to use personal information to make a decision, or to do something substantially and directly related to making a decision, and that decision could reasonably be expected to significantly affect the rights or interests of an individual, the entity's privacy policy must include:

  • the kinds of personal information used in the operation of the computer program; and
  • the kinds of such decisions made solely by the operation of the program; and
  • the kinds of such decisions for which a thing, substantially and directly related to making the decision, is done by the operation of the program.

In plainer words, the policy must tell people what information feeds the automated process and which kinds of decisions the automation makes or helps to make. It is a transparency rule. It does not ban automated decisions, and it does not require you to explain a particular decision in the way some overseas laws do.

Who has to do it?

The Privacy Act applies to most businesses with annual turnover above $3 million and to smaller businesses in certain categories, such as health service providers and businesses that trade in personal information. The new rule only matters to those covered entities. A small business that is not covered by the Act does not have to add the clause, though some choose to be transparent anyway.

Not sure if you are covered? Our free Privacy Act checker takes about two minutes.

What counts as a decision that significantly affects someone

The Act does not give a list, but the explanatory material that came with the reform points to examples such as decisions about whether someone gets a benefit, decisions that affect a person's rights under a contract or arrangement, and decisions about access to significant services or support. A one-off marketing email is unlikely to qualify. A system that refuses a customer credit, rejects a job applicant, cancels a service or sets whether someone is offered a product probably could.

The words substantially and directly related matter. If a program scores an applicant and a human merely approves whatever it says, the automation may still be doing the thing that matters. A real review, where a person can and does change the outcome, is different. Be honest when you decide which category you are in.

A quick test for your business

  1. List every tool that uses customer, staff or applicant information to sort, score, approve or reject people.
  2. For each, ask: does it make or shape a decision about someone's access to something, such as a service, a price, a job, a refund or a contract?
  3. Ask: could that reasonably be expected to significantly affect them?
  4. If yes, write down the kinds of information it uses and the kind of decision it makes.
  5. If no, record why. A short note on file is useful if anyone asks later.

Wording examples

Every example below is illustrative. Replace the details with what your systems actually do. Do not copy a clause that describes something you do not do, because an inaccurate privacy policy is its own problem.

Example 1: no covered automated decisions

If you have checked and nothing qualifies, you do not need a clause. Some businesses add a short, true statement for clarity:

We do not use automated systems to make decisions that significantly affect individuals. Where we use software to help us organise enquiries, a person at our business reviews and decides any outcome.

Only say this if it is accurate. Writing it commits you to it.

Example 2: a lender or finance broker using a scoring tool

We use a computer program to help assess applications. The program uses information you give us, such as your income, expenses, employment details and credit information. It produces a risk score that we use to decide whether to approve an application and on what terms. In some cases the program recommends a decision and a member of our team reviews it before we confirm it.

Example 3: a recruiter or employer screening applicants

We use software to sort job applications. It uses the information in your résumé and application, such as your work history, qualifications and answers to screening questions, to rank applicants against the role. The ranking helps us decide who to interview. A person makes the final decision about who is interviewed and hired.

Example 4: an online business that automatically accepts or declines orders

We use automated fraud checks on orders. The checks use details such as your name, delivery address, email address, device information and payment details to decide whether to accept an order. If an order is declined, you can contact us and a person will review the decision.

Example 5: a service business using AI to triage enquiries

We use an automated assistant to answer calls and web enquiries, take details and book callbacks. It uses the information you give us, such as your name, phone number and a description of the job. It does not decide whether we will accept you as a customer. Our staff make that decision.

This is the example most trade and service businesses will recognise. An assistant that books callbacks is unlikely to make a decision that significantly affects anyone, and the paragraph is more about openness than a legal requirement. If your assistant ever starts to decide who gets service, the answer changes.

Where to put it and how to keep it honest

  • Put the clause in the privacy policy, not hidden in the terms. APP 1 is about the privacy policy.
  • Use plain words. If your customers cannot understand it, it is not transparent.
  • Describe kinds of information and kinds of decisions, not every field and every rule.
  • Keep a short internal register of tools that make or support decisions, and review it when you add one.
  • Update the policy when the system changes. A policy that describes last year's process is a risk.

What happens if you do nothing?

A privacy policy that does not meet APP 1 is a compliance gap, and the Office of the Australian Information Commissioner (OAIC) can investigate and, for serious or repeated interferences with privacy, seek civil penalties. In practice, the likelier consequence for a small business is a customer, a client or an insurer asking to see your policy and finding it out of date. A short, accurate update before the date is cheap by comparison.
