Data Breach Email to Customers: AU Template

← Back to the archive
logged 28 November 2026 · 9 min read

A lost laptop, a hacked email account, a spreadsheet sent to the wrong person. At some point most businesses have to write the email that starts: we need to tell you about a problem with your information. It is a hard email to write under stress, so this guide gives you a template to adapt, explains what Australian law requires the notice to contain, and lists what to do in the first 24 hours.

This is general information, not legal advice. If a serious breach has happened, contact your lawyer and insurer straight away.

Does the Notifiable Data Breaches scheme apply to you?

The Notifiable Data Breaches (NDB) scheme sits in Part IIIC of the Privacy Act 1988. It applies to organisations covered by the Act: broadly, businesses with annual turnover above $3 million, and smaller ones that fall into an exception, such as health service providers, businesses that trade in personal information, and some contracted service providers. It also covers credit reporting bodies, credit providers and tax file number recipients regardless of size.

If you are a small business outside those categories, the scheme does not strictly bind you. Writing to affected customers is still the decent and sensible thing to do. It may also be required by a contract, a client agreement or your insurance policy, so read those before you decide it is optional.

When is a breach notifiable?

An eligible data breach happens when three things are true:

  1. There has been unauthorised access to, or unauthorised disclosure of, personal information, or personal information has been lost in circumstances where unauthorised access or disclosure is likely.
  2. A reasonable person would conclude the breach is likely to result in serious harm to one or more of the people the information is about.
  3. The business has not been able to prevent the likely risk of serious harm with remedial action.

Serious harm can be physical, psychological, emotional, financial or reputational. Examples include identity fraud, financial loss and risk to someone's safety. A misdirected email to one trusted contact may not meet the bar. A stolen file of names, addresses, dates of birth and licence numbers likely does.

If you only suspect a breach, you must carry out a reasonable and expeditious assessment, and the Act sets 30 days as the outer limit for completing it. If you decide it is eligible, you must notify the Office of the Australian Information Commissioner (OAIC) and the affected people as soon as practicable.

What the notice must contain

The statement you give to the OAIC, and the notice to individuals, must include:

  • your identity and contact details;
  • a description of the breach that you believe has happened;
  • the kinds of information involved;
  • recommendations about the steps individuals should take in response.

There are three ways to notify individuals: tell everyone whose information was involved, tell only those at risk of serious harm, or, if neither is practicable, publish a statement on your website and take reasonable steps to publicise it. Email is a common channel, but use whatever you normally use to reach that person.

A data breach notification email template

Replace anything in square brackets. Keep the sentences short. Do not guess: if you do not know something, say so and say when you will know more.

Subject: Important: a security incident affecting your information
Dear [First name],
We are writing to tell you about a security incident at [Business name] that involved some of your personal information. We are sorry this has happened and we want you to know exactly what we know so far.
What happened: On [date], we discovered that [short description, for example, an unauthorised person gained access to one of our email accounts between date and date]. We [stopped the access / secured the account / engaged an IT specialist] on [date].
What information was involved: The information included your [name, email address, phone number, address, date of birth]. It did not include [payment card numbers / passwords / identity documents], as far as we can tell at this stage. [If unknown: We are still working out exactly which details were affected and will update you by date.]
What we are doing: We have [reset passwords and turned on multi-factor authentication / removed access / notified the Office of the Australian Information Commissioner / reported the incident to the police via ReportCyber]. We are also reviewing how this happened so it does not happen again.
What you can do: Be careful with any message that claims to be from us and asks for payment or login details. We will not ask you for your password or card number by email, text or phone. If you use the same password anywhere else, change it. Check bank and online accounts for activity you do not recognise, and contact your bank if you see any. You can also get free support from IDCARE, Australia's national identity and cyber support service, at idcare.org.
Who to contact: Please call [Name] on [phone number] or email [email address] if you have questions. We will reply as quickly as we can.
Again, we are sorry. We take the security of your information seriously and we will keep you informed.
[Name, role, Business name, ABN]

Why the template reads this way

  • It says sorry once, plainly. Over-apologising makes the email longer and harder to use.
  • It leads with facts. People want to know what happened, what was taken and what to do.
  • It gives a phone number. A breach email with no human contact is the quickest way to damage trust.
  • It tells people what you will never ask for. Scammers often pounce on breach news with fake follow-up messages.
  • It does not link to a login page. Telling people to click a link and sign in is exactly what a phishing email does. If they need a website, name it in plain text and let them type it in.

What to leave out

  • Speculation about who did it or why.
  • Minimising phrases such as no evidence of misuse, if you have not checked.
  • Blame on a supplier or staff member. Say what happened, not who is at fault.
  • Technical jargon the reader will not understand.
  • A promise you cannot keep, such as your data is now completely safe.

The first 24 hours

  1. Stop the harm: lock accounts, change passwords, switch on multi-factor authentication, disconnect affected devices.
  2. Write down what you know, with times. Keep logs and messages. You will need them.
  3. Call your insurer if you have cyber cover. Many policies require prompt notice and may provide an incident response line.
  4. Report to police through ReportCyber at cyber.gov.au if it was a cyber crime.
  5. Work out what information was involved and whose it was.
  6. Decide whether the breach is eligible under the NDB scheme and, if so, prepare the OAIC statement and the notice to individuals.
  7. Brief your staff on what to say if customers call, and who handles calls.
  8. Warn customers about scam follow-ups.

Prevention is cheaper

Most breaches among small businesses start with email: a compromised mailbox, a faked invoice, a stolen password. Two controls help most: multi-factor authentication on every email account, and a properly set up email domain so nobody can easily send messages that look like they came from you.
