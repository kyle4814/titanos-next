Quote Follow-Up Text Message Examples for Tradies

← Back to the archive
logged 28 November 2026 · 9 min read

You sent the quote on Monday. It is now Thursday and you have heard nothing. Do you text, ring, or wait? Most trade businesses wait, because chasing feels pushy and the next job is already on. That silence is where a lot of winnable work goes to die. A short, friendly text a couple of days after the quote is the cheapest sales activity a tradie has.

This guide gives you the messages themselves, written the way a tradie in Brisbane or Bendigo would actually talk, along with the rhythm we suggest and the few rules that keep you out of trouble. Everything here is a starting point. Change the words until they sound like you, because customers can tell when a text was written by someone else.

Why a text, and why it works

A text gets read. It sits on the phone until the customer looks at it, it does not need a reply to be useful, and it is easy to answer with one word. An email can land in a folder nobody opens. A phone call catches people at work and they often do not pick up from an unknown number. A text is the polite middle ground, and for a small quote it matches how people already talk to tradies.

There is a second reason. Customers who go quiet are usually not saying no. They are comparing, waiting on a partner, or simply forgot. A follow-up that is easy to answer gives them a way back in. That is the idea behind our earlier post on dead quotes, which looks at how to put a rough dollar figure on the quotes sitting unanswered in your system.

If you want to see what your own pile might be worth, start with the dead quote post and then come back here for the wording.

A cadence you can defend

There is no rule that says how many follow-ups are right. This is the rhythm we suggest for a typical residential or small commercial quote. Adjust it for job size: a bathroom renovation deserves more patience than a gutter clean.

  1. Day 0: send the quote with a one-line text so it does not get lost in email.
  2. Day 2 or 3: a friendly check-in that asks if they have questions.
  3. Day 7: a useful follow-up with a reason to reply, such as your next available start date.
  4. Day 14: a polite last nudge that makes it easy to say no.
  5. After that, stop. If they did not reply to four touches, a fifth will not help and may annoy them.

Stopping is part of the plan. A clean, courteous final message leaves the door open and is often the one that gets the reply.

Message examples you can copy

Day 0: sending the quote

Hi Sarah, it is Mick from Coastal Electrical. I have emailed the quote for the switchboard upgrade. It is also a PDF if the email does not show up. Any questions, just reply here.

Day 2 or 3: the friendly check-in

Hi Sarah, Mick from Coastal Electrical. Just checking the quote arrived and made sense. Happy to go through any line of it if that helps.

This works because it asks about the quote, not for a decision. People find it easy to say yes it arrived and they will often add a question.

Day 7: give them a reason to reply

Hi Sarah, Mick here. I have a gap the week of the 15th if you would like to lock the switchboard job in. No pressure either way, but I wanted you to have the date.

Notice that the date is true. Never invent scarcity. If you do not have a real gap, say something else, such as that you are happy to hold the price until the end of the month.

Day 14: the polite last nudge

Hi Sarah, Mick from Coastal Electrical. I will assume the switchboard job is on the back burner, which is fine. If you would still like it done, just say and I will pencil you in. Either way, thanks for considering us.

When the customer says it is too expensive

Thanks for being straight with me, Sarah. I can look at where the cost sits. If you want, I can split it into stages so the safety work is done first and the rest waits.

A price objection is a conversation starter, not a rejection. Offer a smaller scope or a staged job before you offer a lower rate. Cutting your price straight away trains customers to ask.

When they got another quote

No worries, Sarah. Thanks for letting me know. If the other quote has different inclusions to mine, I am happy to compare line by line so you are comparing like with like.

When they said they would think about it

Hi Sarah, I know you wanted to think it over. Is there anything I can clear up that would help you decide? Even a quick yes or no is useful for my scheduling.

Commercial or strata jobs

Hi Priya, Mick from Coastal Electrical following up on the quote for the common area lighting. Does the committee need anything further, such as insurance certificates or a licence number? I can send those today.

With strata and commercial customers the quote often waits on a committee meeting or an approval chain. Asking what they need is more useful than asking if they are ready.

What makes a follow-up text good

  • It names the job. Switchboard upgrade is better than your quote.
  • It uses their name and yours, and the business name, so they know who is texting.
  • It asks one thing. Do you have questions beats a list of three requests.
  • It reads like a person. If it sounds like a template, rewrite it.
  • It is short enough to read in one glance on a lock screen.

What to avoid

  • Guilt or pressure, such as I have turned other work down for you.
  • Fake deadlines. If the price really lapses, say when and why. If not, do not claim it.
  • Texting at night or on weekends unless the customer wrote to you then.
  • Chasing for weeks. Stop after the last nudge.
  • Mixing a follow-up with advertising. A quote reminder is one thing, a promotion for another service is another.

The rules that apply

A follow-up to someone who asked you for a quote is a reply within an existing relationship. The Spam Act 2003 regulates commercial electronic messages and expects them to identify the sender and, for marketing, include an opt-out. We recommend putting your business name in every message and adding a STOP line to anything beyond the first check-in. Honour a STOP straight away.

We go through the details, and the consent rules, in our Spam Act guide for missed call text back. The same logic applies to quote follow-ups.

If you store customer names and numbers in a job app or a spreadsheet, treat them with care. Do not paste a whole customer list into an AI tool you have not checked. The Privacy Act 1988 applies to businesses above $3 million turnover and some smaller ones, and a good habit protects you either way.

Not sure whether it applies to you? Try the Privacy Act checker.

Using software or AI to do the sending

You can send these by hand in five minutes a day. If volume grows, two sensible options exist. The first is the follow-up feature in your job software. ServiceM8, Tradify and simPRO each have some form of reminder or automation, so check what yours does before buying anything new. The second is an AI assistant that drafts the message and waits for you to approve it. We prefer that approach for small businesses, because nothing goes out under your name that you have not seen.

Track it so you know it works

Write down how many quotes you sent last month, how many got a reply to a follow-up, and how many became jobs. Do the same next month with the follow-ups running. Compare the two. If your numbers are small, the sample is small, so do not read too much into one month. The point is to replace a feeling with a number.
