## One job, not ten

With a week of numbers in front of you, the temptation is to fix everything at once. Please do not. Pick one job. A small win that you can see and measure will teach you more than a big plan that stalls.

## What makes a good first job

Look at your grouped log and test each repeat job against these questions.

- Does it follow roughly the same pattern each time? Chasing an unanswered quote does. Negotiating a complicated contract does not.
- Is the output words? Drafts of emails, texts, summaries and checklists are what AI tools do well.
- Is there a clear finish line? "Every quote older than five days has had one follow-up" is a finish line. "Better customer service" is not.
- Is the cost of a mistake small and catchable? A draft you read before sending is safe. Anything that moves money or makes a promise on its own is not a good first job.
- Do you have the information to hand? The tool can only work with what you give it.

Good first jobs for an office of 2 to 20 people often include quote follow-ups, polite payment reminders, sorting the inbox into what needs a reply today, turning meeting notes into an action list, and a welcome message for new clients.

## Poor first jobs

Leave these for later, or leave them with a person for good: anything involving a dispute, a complaint, a sensitive staff matter, a legal notice, or advice you are professionally responsible for. These need human judgement and human accountability.

## Write the job down

Describe the job in one short paragraph so anyone could pick it up. Use this shape.

> "The job is [what]. It starts when [trigger]. It ends when [finish line]. The information needed is [list]. A person called [name] reads and approves every draft before it goes out."

Notice the last line. The approver is a named person, not "the team".

## Test it without risk

Before you change your real routine, run the job once as a trial using a made-up example or one with names and details removed. Ask your AI assistant for a draft and compare it with what you would normally write. Ask yourself three questions. Is it accurate? Does it sound like us? Would I be comfortable if the client saw it?

If the answer to any is no, adjust the instructions, not your standards. You can say:

> "That was too formal. Write it again in a warm, short, plain style, like a friendly local business, and keep it under eighty words."

## Score it

The first job to hand over scorecard worksheet lets you compare two or three candidate jobs side by side. Score each honestly and pick the highest. If two tie, pick the one that people in the office find most annoying. Relief is a good tiebreaker.
