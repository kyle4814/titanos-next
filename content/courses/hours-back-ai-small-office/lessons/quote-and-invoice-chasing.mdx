## The two awkward messages

Two messages cause more quiet dread in small offices than almost any others: "Did you get my quote?" and "Just a reminder about that invoice." Both are polite, both are repetitive, and both get put off. Putting them off costs you either work or cash flow. That makes them a very good first job for a drafting tool.

## A simple rhythm

Pick a rhythm and write it down. You decide the gaps, because you know your customers. A three-step shape works well for both quotes and invoices.

1. A friendly check-in that makes it easy to reply.
2. A clear, kind nudge with the key facts repeated.
3. A direct but respectful final message that says what happens next.

For invoices, steps follow your payment terms. Check your own terms and contracts first, because what you can say or do about late payment depends on them. If you are unsure, ask your accountant or a lawyer.

## Prompts to copy

Give the tool the facts it needs and say what you do not want.

> "Draft a short, friendly text message to a customer who has not replied to a quote we sent on [date] for [job]. Ask if they have any questions. Do not offer a discount. Do not pressure them. Under fifty words."

> "Draft a polite email reminding a client that invoice [number] for [amount] was due on [date]. Keep it calm and warm. State how to pay. Do not threaten anything. Leave a space in square brackets for anything you are unsure of."

> "Now write step two, a little firmer but still courteous, for the same client."

Notice that you supply the number, amount and date from your own records. Never let the tool work these out or recall them. After the draft arrives, check each one against your accounting system.

## Keep a tracking sheet

A simple spreadsheet beats memory. Columns that help: client, date sent, amount, step reached, date of last message, who approved it, outcome. This is also where the time saving shows up, because you no longer search your sent folder to find out who has been chased.

## Know your relationships

A tool does not know that a particular client is going through a hard time, or that an invoice is late because of your own mistake. You do. Read each draft with that person in mind and soften, wait or phone instead. For a payment plan or a hardship conversation, speak to the person yourself.

## The honest limits

Drafts can get a figure wrong, address the wrong person, or be firmer than you meant. A message that goes to the wrong client with the wrong amount is far more costly than the minutes you saved. This is why the approver reads every word, every time, before anything is sent.
