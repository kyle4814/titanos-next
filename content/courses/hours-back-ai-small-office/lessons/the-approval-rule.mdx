## Write the rule before you use the tool

The most important page in your whole AI routine is a short one you write yourself: the approval rule. It says what the AI may do, what it may not, and who signs off. Write it now, before anything goes out under your business name.

## The principle

AI drafts. A human approves every message. That sentence has teeth only if it is written down, because busy days wear away good intentions. When you are tired, a draft that looks fine is very tempting to send unread. A rule on paper stops that.

## A starting rule you can copy

Adapt this to your office. Keep it to one page.

1. AI tools may draft text for us. They may not send anything on their own.
2. A named person reads every draft in full before it is sent, and changes anything that is wrong or does not sound like us.
3. We check every name, date, amount and promise against our own records.
4. We never paste sensitive material into an AI tool (see the privacy lesson).
5. If a draft is about money in dispute, a complaint, a staff matter or anything legal, a person writes it from scratch.
6. If we are not sure, we do not send it.
7. The owner can change or stop any of this at any time.

## Who approves

Name one approver for each job and one backup for sick days. The approver should be someone who knows the client or the matter. A new starter can draft the request, but the person who understands the relationship should sign off on tone.

Make approval visible: the approver replaces the word "DRAFT" at the top with their initials before sending.

## Tell your team

People reasonably worry that new tools mean fewer jobs. Say plainly that nobody is being replaced and the aim is to give hours back. Show the rule, and ask what to hand over first, because the people doing the work know where it hurts.

## Tell the AI the rule too

You can build the rule into the way you ask. A reusable opening line looks like this.

> "You are helping a small Australian office draft messages. You only write drafts for a person to review. Use plain Australian English. If you do not know a fact, say so and leave a clear gap marked in square brackets instead of guessing."

That last instruction matters. A tool that fills a gap with an invented date or amount is more dangerous than one that leaves a blank you must fill.

## The honest limit

AI drafts can be confident and wrong. They can invent a detail, mix up two clients, or promise something you never offered. The rule does not remove that risk, it catches it. Approval is the safety, so treat it as the actual job rather than a formality.
