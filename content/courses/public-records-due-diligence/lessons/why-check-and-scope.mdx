## The worry behind every signature

You are about to sign with a supplier, accept a big order from a new buyer, or agree to a partnership. Something in the back of your mind says: do I actually know who these people are? Most small teams have no way to answer that, so they sign anyway and hope.

This course gives you a calm, repeatable way to answer it. Everything here uses public records only. You will not log in to anything that is not yours, you will not contact the organisation, and you will not pretend to be anyone. This is not legal advice, you stay in control of every decision, and a small team can do the whole check in an afternoon.

## What due diligence means here

Due diligence is a short, fair look at what is publicly known about an organisation before you commit. It does not prove an organisation is good. It finds out whether anything public contradicts what they have told you, and whether anything public should make you slow down.

Think of it as reading the sign on the door, the licence on the wall and the notices on the noticeboard. All of it is already out in the open.

## Scope first

Before you search anything, decide what you are checking and why. A check with no scope turns into an evening of idle browsing and a pile of notes nobody can use.

Write down four things.

- **The counterparty.** The exact legal name, any trading names, and the ABN they gave you.
- **The decision.** For example: sign a 12 month supply agreement, pay a deposit, share customer data, or take on a subcontractor.
- **The exposure.** What you lose if it goes badly. Think money, time, reputation and data.
- **The depth.** A light check for a small, reversible decision. A fuller check where the exposure is large or hard to undo.

Scope also protects you ethically. If you write down the decision and the reason for the check, you can show later that you looked at an organisation for a legitimate business purpose and nothing more.

## One name, many entities

Many small organisations trade under a name that is not the legal name of the company you would sign with. A trust, a company and a sole trader can all sit behind one shopfront. Scope must name the exact entity that will be on the contract, because that is the entity whose record matters.

## Do this now

Pick one organisation you already deal with, such as a supplier you pay monthly or a buyer you invoice.

1. Write its legal name, trading name and ABN from your last invoice or contract.
2. Write the decision the check would support, for example renewing or extending terms.
3. Write what you would lose if it went wrong.
4. Choose light or full depth, and note why.
5. Open the scope and source tracker worksheet and fill in the first section.
