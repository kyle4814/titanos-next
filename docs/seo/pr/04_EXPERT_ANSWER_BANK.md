## AI automation (5)
1. Q: What should a small business automate first with AI? A: Start with the repetitive message that costs you a job when it is late: replying to a missed call, chasing a quote, sending an invoice reminder. Pick one, write the exact message a human would send, test it on ten real cases, and keep a person approving until it is right. Sources: none needed (practice advice); do not quote a savings figure unless the owner measured it.
2. Q: Will AI replace small-business staff? A: In the practices I work with the aim is hours back, not heads gone: the tool does the first draft or the first reply and the person keeps the judgement and the relationship. I would not claim a job-loss figure either way; measure hours returned on your own jobs. Sources: none; no statistics stated.
3. Q: Is it safe to put customer details into an AI tool? A: Only if you know where the data goes. Check the vendor's terms on training and retention, use a business plan rather than a free consumer one, and leave out health, payment and identity details unless the tool is built for them. Sources: OAIC guidance on commercially available AI products [V, check the current OAIC page title before citing]; Act24.
4. Q: How do I tell if an AI automation is worth it? A: Time ten manual runs, cost the tool for a month, and compare per task. If you cannot name the task and the saving in one sentence, do not buy it yet. Sources: none; method only.
5. Q: Should an AI answer my business phone? A: It can take a message and book a call-back, but tell callers it is automated, keep a clear route to a human, and check call-recording consent rules in your state before recording. Sources: state surveillance-devices laws, e.g. Queensland Invasion of Privacy Act 1971 [V], plus the Act24 transparency changes below.

## Privacy Act (4)
6. Q: Does the Privacy Act apply to my small business? A: Many small businesses with turnover of $3 million or less are exempt, but the exemption has exceptions, such as health service providers, businesses that trade in personal information, and those that opt in or are contracted to the government. Check your own position before assuming. This is general information, not legal advice. Sources: Privacy Act 1988 s 6D and OAIC small business page [V].
7. Q: What changes on 10 December 2026? A: Privacy policies of covered organisations must describe the kinds of personal information used in, and the decisions made by, computer programs that substantially do the deciding where the decision could significantly affect someone. The date is two years from the Act's Royal Assent on 10 December 2024. Confirm the commencement table before relying on it. General information, not legal advice. Sources: Act24 [F]; OAIC statement on the bill's passage [F, via search].
8. Q: What if I have a data breach? A: If you are covered by the Act and an eligible breach is likely to cause serious harm, the scheme requires a prompt assessment and then notification to the OAIC and affected people; fix the cause, keep a record, and ask your IT provider for logs on day one. General information, not legal advice. Sources: OAIC Notifiable Data Breaches scheme pages [V].
9. Q: What should a small business put in a privacy policy? A: What you collect, why, who sees it, where it is stored, how someone asks for a copy or a correction, and, from 10 December 2026 where it applies, how automated decisions are used. Write it so a customer can read it in two minutes. General information, not legal advice. Sources: APP 1, OAIC [V]; Act24 [F].

## Essential Eight (3)
10. Q: What is the Essential Eight and do small businesses need it? A: It is the Australian Signals Directorate's baseline of eight mitigation strategies, with four maturity levels from zero to three. It is written for government and large organisations first, but small businesses can use it as a checklist, starting with multi-factor authentication, patching and tested backups. Sources: E8 [F, via search]; ASD maturity model, November 2023 update [F, via search].
11. Q: Where should a five-person business start? A: Turn on multi-factor authentication for email and banking, switch on automatic updates, and test a restore from backup this week. Those three cover the controls most likely to stop an everyday compromise, and each takes under an hour. Sources: E8; ACSC-SB [V].
12. Q: Can I get certified in the Essential Eight? A: There is no certificate; ASD publishes the model and an assessment process, and an assessor rates your maturity level. Be wary of anyone selling a "certificate". Sources: ASD assessment process guide, via cyber.gov.au [F, via search].

## Email security (3)
13. Q: How do criminals fake a small business's email? A: With no enforced DMARC policy, a receiving mail server has no instruction to reject mail that merely claims your domain. In a passive sample of 318 small .com.au domains I took on 30 September and 1 October 2026, about 48 per cent had no enforced policy and no security.txt file; it is a floor, not a census. Sources: Study; RFC 7489 [V].
14. Q: What can a small business do about invoice-redirect scams today? A: Make one rule: no change to bank details without a phone call to a number you already hold. Add DMARC at "quarantine" once reports are clean, turn on multi-factor authentication, and tell the accountant the rule. DMARC does not stop lookalike domains. Sources: ACSC-SB [V]; Study; Scamwatch (https://www.scamwatch.gov.au) [V].
15. Q: What is DMARC in plain English? A: A published rule that tells receiving mail servers what to do with email that claims to come from your domain but fails checks: nothing (monitor), hold it, or reject it. Start at monitor, read the reports for a few weeks, then move to quarantine. Sources: RFC 7489 [V]; Study.
