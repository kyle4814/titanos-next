Government tenders

Government Tenders for Small Business in Australia: Where to Start

By Kyle Deligny, Brisbane. Last reviewed 28 November 2026. About 11 minute read. General information, not legal advice.

Governments in Australia buy an enormous range of goods and services, from cleaning, trades and landscaping to IT support, training and consulting, and a good share of it is open to small businesses. The process looks forbidding from outside: unfamiliar portals, long documents, scoring criteria and compliance forms. In practice it is a learnable routine, and small firms win work every week by being ready, selective and clear.

This guide covers where tenders are published, how Commonwealth, state and local buying works at a high level, what to have ready before you bid, how to decide whether a tender is worth your time, how to read the documents, how panels and subcontracting offer an easier way in, and the mistakes that sink first bids. It is general information based on public sources, with links to each, and it makes no promise that you will win anything.

Awards are intelligence, not opportunities

AusTender publishes both open approaches to market, which you can bid on, and awarded contract notices, which you cannot. Awarded notices are still valuable: they show who buys what, at what value and who the incumbent is. Do not confuse the two lists.

Where tenders are published

There is no single national list. Each level of government has its own portal, and some buyers use private procurement platforms. A small business should know the ones that match where it works.

How Commonwealth buying works, in outline

Commonwealth entities follow the Commonwealth Procurement Rules (CPRs), issued by the Department of Finance. The core principle is value for money, not lowest price. Non-corporate Commonwealth entities have additional obligations above set thresholds, including a requirement to approach the market openly for many procurements. The threshold at the time of writing is 80,000 dollars for most goods and services and more for construction, but the current figures are in the CPRs, so check there.

The common approaches to market you will see:

  • Open tender. Published for anyone to respond.
  • Limited tender. The buyer invites selected suppliers, or uses a panel, in the circumstances the rules allow.
  • Panel or standing offer. A list of pre-approved suppliers. Once on it, you compete for work among panel members. Joining often happens in a defined window every few years.
  • Request for quote. For smaller procurements, a simpler process with fewer documents.

Commonwealth contracts valued at 10,000 dollars or more are reported on AusTender through contract notices. That gives the public view you can use for research, such as who won, the value and the period.

The Commonwealth also has policies to help small and medium businesses, including measures to pay eligible small business invoices quickly. The Department of Finance's procurement pages are the source for the current policies and thresholds. States and local governments have their own thresholds and rules, which differ in detail, and you must read the document in front of you.

What to have ready before you bid

Most first-bid failures are not about price or quality. They are about missing paperwork found at the last minute. Build a bid library once and reuse it.

  1. ABN and GST registration details, and your entity type. Verify your listing at ABN Lookup.
  2. Insurance certificates: public liability, professional indemnity where relevant, and workers compensation. Buyers set minimum cover, so know your limits.
  3. Licences and qualifications for your trade or field, and for staff who will do the work.
  4. A capability statement: one or two pages describing what you do, who you have done it for, your key people, your certifications and contact details.
  5. Referees and past work: three or more clients who will take a call, with short, specific descriptions. Use real ones only.
  6. Policies: work health and safety, quality, privacy, and incident reporting, at a size that suits your business.
  7. Financial information: some tenders ask for financial viability evidence. Have your accountant ready.
  8. Security evidence: a short statement of your cyber practices. For the Essential Eight, know your approximate maturity and have evidence of MFA, backups and updates.
  9. Business continuity and key people cover in a paragraph.
  10. Standard answers to the questions that recur: methodology, risk, quality assurance, supply chain, safety.

A capability statement is the one document that does the most work. Keep it factual, include numbers you can prove, and add a line on how you meet each category buyers ask about. Do not copy a template from another business unchanged.

Should you bid at all? A scoring method

Bidding costs time. A serious response can take several days, and a no-bid decision made quickly is a good decision. Score each tender from 0 to 2 on these questions.

Bid or no-bid scorecard
Question012
Can we meet every mandatory requirement today?NoWith effortYes
Have we done very similar work, with a referee?NoSomewhatYes
Do we know the buyer or have a relationship?NoSlightlyYes
Is the contract size right for our capacity?Too large or too smallStretchComfortable
Is the timeline and location workable?NoTightYes
Is price the only criterion, or does quality count?Price onlyMixedQuality weighted
Can we make acceptable margin at the likely price?NoThinYes
Is the incumbent obviously entrenched?YesMaybeNo or none

Add the scores. Fourteen or more: bid. Ten to thirteen: bid only if you can reuse most of your library. Under ten: skip. The point of the method is to protect your time, because winning one in three well-chosen bids beats losing nine in ten scattergun ones.

A no is information too

Record why you did not bid. After ten tenders you will see which categories and buyers suit you, and you will bid better.

Reading a tender without getting lost

Tender documents vary, but most have the same parts. Read them in this order.

  1. The summary or request: what the buyer wants, the contract period, the value if given, and the closing date and time. Check the time zone.
  2. Conditions of tender: how to submit, whether late bids are rejected, questions deadlines and the contact person.
  3. Mandatory requirements: pass or fail items. Missing one usually means your bid is not assessed.
  4. Evaluation criteria and weightings: where your points come from. If price is 30 per cent and capability 70 per cent, spend your effort accordingly.
  5. Draft contract: insurance, liability, intellectual property, payment terms, termination. Flag anything you cannot accept.
  6. Response schedules: the forms and pricing templates you must complete.

Use the questions period. Buyers answer questions in writing and share the answers with all bidders. A good question clarifies scope or the evaluation without giving away your plan. Do not ask something the document already answers.

Write every response to the criterion, not about yourself. If the criterion is "demonstrated experience in similar projects", the answer is two or three specific projects with scope, value, outcome and a referee, not a company history.

Writing a bid that scores

  • Answer every question, in the order asked, using the buyer's headings.
  • Be specific. "We completed 14 school sites in 2025 on time and under budget" scores better than "we have extensive experience", provided it is true and you can name a referee.
  • Show how, not just that. Evaluators want method, people, timelines and risk handling.
  • Price clearly. Complete the schedule exactly. Hidden assumptions and unexplained gaps cost points. Check the arithmetic.
  • Stay inside the limits. If the word limit is 500, write 500.
  • Proof and edit. Have someone who did not write it read it cold.
  • Submit early. Portals fail near deadlines. Aim to be done a day before.
  • Do not misstate. Overstating experience or capacity can disqualify you or cause trouble during the contract.

AI tools can help with structure, checking against criteria and tightening wording, but you must supply the facts and check every line. A bid written by a tool that has not been given your real projects and numbers reads generic and scores badly. Many tenders also ask whether AI was used. If so, answer truthfully.

Panels and subcontracting: the easier door

A first prime contract is the hardest path. Two others are friendlier.

Panels

A panel is a list of approved suppliers. Getting on one involves a larger application, but afterwards you can be invited to quote for jobs without a full open tender each time. Panels often reopen periodically, so keep an eye on the dates. Councils, state departments and large companies all use them. Ask each buyer how and when they refresh theirs.

Subcontracting

Large contractors win government work and then need local trades and services to deliver it. Awarded contract notices on AusTender and state portals show who won, what, and where. A short, specific note to the winner offering a capability they need, such as a licensed trade in the region, is a legitimate way to get work and build a track record. Subcontracting gives you referees and past-performance examples, which is exactly what a first prime tender asks for.

Security and privacy questions you will meet

Many tenders now include a security schedule. Common asks are multi-factor authentication, patching, backups, data location, incident reporting and privacy compliance. The ACSC's Essential Eight is the usual reference. Work-from-home staff, subcontractors and cloud tools are all in scope for these questions.

If your work touches personal information on the buyer's behalf, you may be treated as a contracted service provider, and the Privacy Act can apply to that work even if you are otherwise exempt. See the Privacy Act guide. Answer honestly, and state what you can evidence today and what you will do by when.

Email security is a cheap quick win: a business that cannot show a DMARC policy looks careless on a security questionnaire. See email security for small business.

Common first-bid mistakes

  • Bidding on everything and winning nothing.
  • Missing a mandatory item, such as an insurance certificate or a signed form.
  • Answering the question you wish had been asked.
  • Pricing below cost to "get a foot in the door" and then losing money for years.
  • Copying a competitor's language or a template from the internet without matching it to your business.
  • Leaving the submission to the last hour.
  • Not asking for feedback after a loss. Most buyers will give a debrief if you ask politely, and it is the cheapest training you can get.

A first 90 days

  1. Weeks 1 to 2: write your capability statement, gather insurances, licences and three referees into one folder.
  2. Weeks 3 to 4: set saved searches and alerts on AusTender and your state portal. Read ten awarded notices in your category to learn who buys and at what size.
  3. Weeks 5 to 8: read five live tenders end to end without bidding. Score them with the scorecard above.
  4. Weeks 9 to 12: submit one bid, or one panel application, that scores fourteen or more. Ask for feedback whatever the result.

Expect the first win to take longer than three months. Public buyers move on their own calendars, and a no-bid month spent building your library is not wasted.

Questions people ask

Where do I find government tenders in Australia?

The Commonwealth publishes on AusTender. Each state and territory has its own portal, such as QTenders in Queensland and NSW eTendering. Councils publish on their own sites or procurement platforms. Set saved searches and email alerts on the portals that match where you work.

Can a small business win government contracts?

Yes. Governments buy a very wide range of services, many contracts are modest in size, and policies encourage buying from small and medium businesses. Small firms win by being ready, choosing tenders carefully and reusing a strong bid library. There is no guarantee for any single bid.

Do I need to register to see tenders on AusTender?

You can view published approaches to market without paying. Creating a free account lets you save searches and receive notifications. Always follow the instructions in each tender for how to respond, as submission methods vary.

What is a capability statement?

A one or two page summary of what your business does, who it has done it for, its key people, qualifications and certifications and how to contact you. Buyers use it to judge whether to invite or shortlist you, so keep it factual and specific.

What is the difference between a tender and a panel?

A tender is a competition for a specific job or contract. A panel is a list of pre-approved suppliers a buyer can choose from over several years. Joining a panel takes an application, after which you can be invited to quote for individual jobs.

Do I need to meet the Essential Eight to win a government tender?

Not always, but many tenders include a security schedule and ask about it. Know your approximate maturity level and have evidence of MFA, patching and backups. See the Essential Eight guide.

Can I subcontract instead of bidding directly?

Yes, and it is often the easiest way in. Winners of larger contracts need local suppliers. Awarded notices show who won, so you can offer a specific capability. It also builds referees for later bids.
