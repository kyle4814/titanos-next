Privacy Act obligations for a small Australian business

Six parts of the Privacy Act matter most to a 5 to 20 person business: the privacy policy and the new automated decision duty, collection notices, sending data overseas, data breach reporting, the small business exemption and the new privacy tort. Each page below says who it applies to, what it requires, shows a worked example and gives a checklist, with the primary source beside it.

By Kyle Deligny, Titanos, Brisbane. Last reviewed 28 November 2026 against the Privacy Act 1988 (Compilation No. 104, 4 June 2026).

General information, not legal advice. It describes the law as read on the review date for a small business owner. For a decision that matters, ask a privacy lawyer.

Start with the one question that decides everything

Most of the Privacy Act binds an APP entity: an organisation the Act covers. A business with annual turnover of AU$3 million or less is generally a small business operator and sits outside the Australian Privacy Principles, but section 6D then lists exceptions that pull a business back in, and a single year over the line can end the exemption for good. So the first job is not to write a policy. It is to work out whether you are covered. The small business exemption explainer walks through the test, and the free Privacy Act checker asks the same questions in a few minutes.

If you are covered, the next four pages are your working set. If you are exempt, you can still be sued under the statutory tort, and larger customers often write privacy duties into their contracts, so the sixth page and the data breach page are worth reading either way.

The six obligations

  • APP 1 and automated decisions

    From 10 December 2026, an organisation covered by the Privacy Act must add three things to its privacy policy if it has set up a computer program to make, or do something substantially and directly related to making, a decision that could reasonably be expected to significantly affect someone's rights or interests, using their personal information.

    Read the explainer

  • APP 5 notification

    APP 5 requires an organisation covered by the Privacy Act to take reasonable steps, at or before the time it collects personal information (or as soon as practicable afterwards), to tell the person the relevant matters in APP 5.2 or to make sure they are already aware of them.

    Read the explainer

  • APP 8 cross-border

    Before an organisation covered by the Privacy Act discloses personal information to someone outside Australia, it must take reasonable steps to make sure that recipient does not breach the APPs (other than APP 1).

    Read the explainer

  • Notifiable Data Breaches

    Under Part IIIC of the Privacy Act, an organisation covered by the Act that has reasonable grounds to believe an eligible data breach has happened must prepare a statement, give it to the OAIC as soon as practicable, and notify the affected individuals.

    Read the explainer

  • Small business exemption

    A business with annual turnover of AU$3 million or less is a small business, and a small business operator is generally outside the Privacy Act.

    Read the explainer

  • Statutory privacy tort

    Since 10 June 2025, Schedule 2 of the Privacy Act lets an individual sue another person in court if that person intentionally or recklessly invaded their privacy in a serious way, by intruding on their seclusion or misusing information about them, where they had a reasonable expectation of privacy and the public interest in their privacy outweighed any competing public interest.

    Read the explainer

What changed, and what is still coming

  • 10 December 2024. The Privacy and Other Legislation Amendment Act 2024 received Royal Assent. Most of Schedule 1 started the next day, 11 December 2024.
  • 10 June 2025. Schedule 2, the statutory tort for serious invasions of privacy, commenced. The OAIC confirms that date.
  • 10 December 2026. Part 15 of Schedule 1 starts, adding APP 1.7 to 1.9 on automated decisions to privacy policies. At the time of review, the Federal Register showed that date as the start of the next version of the Act.

That is the whole of the dated change for small business this year. Everything else on the six pages has been in force already, so the practical risk for most businesses is an old privacy policy, a missing collection notice, an untracked AI tool or a breach plan that has never been written.

How to use these pages

  1. Work out whether the Act covers you using the exemption explainer or the free checker. Write the answer and the date down.
  2. If covered, list your data: what you collect, where it is stored, which tools touch it and which of those are overseas or use AI.
  3. Fix in order of exposure: privacy policy and collection notices, vendor register for overseas tools, breach response plan, then the automated decision paragraph before 10 December 2026.
  4. Whether or not you are covered, set staff rules on sharing customer information, because the tort turns on conduct.

The privacy template pack in the shop gives you fill-in documents for steps 2 and 3, and Privacy Act compliance with Titanos is the done-with-you route.

Questions people ask

What is the next Privacy Act deadline for a small business?

10 December 2026, when the automated decision transparency duty in APP 1.7 to 1.9 starts. It applies to decisions made after that date, and only to businesses the Act covers.

Does the Privacy Act apply to my business if I turn over less than AU$3 million?

Often not, but it can. Providing a health service, trading in personal information, holding a Commonwealth contract or having earlier exceeded AU$3 million are examples that bring a business within the Act. The small business exemption explainer lists them.

Which of these obligations reach a business that is exempt from the APPs?

On the OAIC's description, the statutory tort for serious invasions of privacy extends beyond APP entities. The other five depend on the business being covered by the Act.
