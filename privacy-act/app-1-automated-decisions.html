APP 1 and the automated decision privacy policy duty

From 10 December 2026, an organisation covered by the Privacy Act must add three things to its privacy policy if it has set up a computer program to make, or do something substantially and directly related to making, a decision that could reasonably be expected to significantly affect someone's rights or interests, using their personal information. It is a disclosure duty. It does not ban automated decisions, and it does not give people a new right to an explanation.

By Kyle Deligny, Titanos, Brisbane. Last reviewed 28 November 2026 against the Privacy Act 1988 (Compilation No. 104, 4 June 2026).

General information, not legal advice. It describes the law as read on the review date for a small business owner. For a decision that matters, ask a privacy lawyer.

Who it applies to

  • Any APP entity. That is an organisation the Act covers (a business over the AU$3 million turnover line, or a smaller business caught by one of the exceptions) or a government agency.
  • A business that has arranged for software to make a decision, or to do a thing substantially and directly related to making it, where personal information is used and the decision could reasonably be expected to significantly affect the person's rights or interests.
  • It applies to decisions made after 10 December 2026, even when the software was set up earlier or the personal information was collected earlier (item 89 of Part 15).

Not sure whether the Act covers your business at all? Start with the small business exemption explainer or run the free checker.

What it requires

What APP 1 already requires

APP 1.3 requires a clearly expressed and up-to-date privacy policy. APP 1.4 lists what it must contain: the kinds of personal information collected and held, how it is collected and held, the purposes, how a person can access and correct it, how to complain, and whether information is likely to go overseas (with the countries where practicable). APP 1.5 says make it available free of charge and in an appropriate form, which is usually the website. APP 1.2 sits underneath: take reasonable steps to put in place practices, procedures and systems that let you comply and handle complaints. None of that changes on 10 December 2026. The new duty is added after APP 1.6.

The new test in APP 1.7

Three conditions must all be true. First, you have arranged for a computer program to make a decision, or to do a thing substantially and directly related to making a decision. Second, the decision could reasonably be expected to significantly affect the rights or interests of an individual. Third, personal information about that individual is used in running the program. The Act says making a decision includes refusing or failing to make one, and that rights or interests can be affected for better or worse (APP 1.9). Its examples are a decision to grant or refuse a benefit, a decision that affects a person's rights under a contract, and a decision that affects access to a significant service or support. The Act does not define 'significantly', so this is a judgement call that you should be able to explain.

What the policy must then say (APP 1.8)

If the test is met, the privacy policy must describe the kinds of personal information used in the operation of those programs, the kinds of decisions made solely by the programs, and the kinds of decisions where the program does a thing substantially and directly related to making the decision (a human still signs off, but the software scored, ranked or filtered). The wording is about kinds, not individual decisions, so a short plain paragraph per tool is the target. Because the duty sits inside the APP 1.4 family, it is judged the same way as the rest of the policy: clearly expressed and up to date.

Worked example: a 14-person trade supplier with online credit accounts

A 14-person Brisbane plumbing supplier is over the AU$3 million line, so the Act covers it. Trade customers apply for a 30-day account online. Since 2025 the supplier has used a scoring add-on in its accounting software: it reads the applicant's name, ABN, director details and trade references, and the add-on either approves the account, declines it, or flags it for the owner to look at. The owner glances at the flagged ones only.

  1. Condition 1: a computer program makes the decision (approve or decline) and, for flagged files, does a thing substantially and directly related to it (the flag). Met.
  2. Condition 2: refusing a credit account affects a person's access to a service and their rights under a contract. A reasonable person would call that significant. Met.
  3. Condition 3: it uses personal information about the director (an individual). Met.
  4. Contrast: the same business uses a chatbot that suggests a delivery window. That has no real effect on anyone's rights or interests, so APP 1.7 is likely not triggered. Keep a one-line note of why you reached that view.
  5. Policy change before 10 December 2026: add a short section naming the kinds of information used (identity, business and credit-reference details), the kind of decision made solely by the software (approve or decline small accounts) and the kind where the software only helps (flagging larger accounts for the owner).

What to take from it. The work is an inventory: list every tool that scores, ranks, filters, approves or declines people, then decide tool by tool whether it meets the three conditions and write the paragraph. For this supplier it is one section of about 120 words and an hour of checking.

The scenario is an illustration written for this page, not a real client or a decision of the regulator.

Checklist

  • Confirm the Act covers you (turnover over AU$3 million, or one of the small business exceptions).
  • List every tool that scores, ranks, filters, approves, declines, prices or schedules people: CRM lead scoring, credit checks, hiring filters, fraud rules, dynamic pricing, roster tools.
  • For each tool, ask the three APP 1.7 questions and write one sentence recording the answer.
  • Ask each vendor in writing what personal information the tool uses and whether a human reviews outcomes.
  • Add the APP 1.8 information to the privacy policy: kinds of information, kinds of decisions made solely by software, kinds of decisions the software only supports.
  • Publish the updated policy before 10 December 2026 and keep the previous version on file.
  • Diary a review whenever a new tool is switched on.

Common traps

  • Reading the duty as 'we use AI, so we must disclose'. The trigger is a decision that significantly affects someone, not the presence of AI.
  • Assuming a human click ends it. A program that does a thing substantially and directly related to the decision is still inside APP 1.7.
  • Leaving it to a vendor's terms. The duty sits on you as the entity that arranged the program.
  • Overreaching: do not claim 'no automated decisions' unless you have checked every tool.

Questions people ask

Does the automated decision duty apply to small businesses?

Only to those the Privacy Act covers. A business with turnover of AU$3 million or less is generally outside the APPs unless an exception in section 6D applies, such as providing a health service or trading in personal information.

Does it ban automated decisions?

No. APP 1.7 and 1.8 only require the privacy policy to say what kinds of information are used and what kinds of decisions are made or supported by software.

Does a decision made before 10 December 2026 count?

The new clause applies to decisions made after the commencement date. It does not matter whether the program was arranged or the personal information was collected earlier.
