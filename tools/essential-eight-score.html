Essential Eight self assessment for small business

Answer 38 plain-English questions and see roughly how close your business is to Maturity Level One of the Essential Eight, with a list of gaps to close and what to ask your IT provider for.

Every question is taken from the Maturity Level One requirements in the ASD Essential Eight maturity model on cyber.gov.au, reworded so an owner can answer it.

What this is and is not. A quick estimate to plan your next steps. It does not scan or test anything, and it is not an ASD assessment, an audit or a certification. Your answers stay in your browser.

0 of 38 answered. Nothing you choose leaves this page.

1

Patch applications

Keep the software people use every day (Microsoft 365, browsers, PDF readers, your website platform) up to date.

Do you have a list of every computer and device on your network, refreshed at least every fortnight?

ASD asks for an automated method of asset discovery, so the list stays current without someone typing it in.

Is your internet-facing software (website, email server, remote access, VPN) scanned for missing patches at least daily?

A vulnerability scanner with an up-to-date database does this. If your website is on a hosted platform, the host may do it for you.

Is everyday software (Office, browsers and their extensions, email clients, PDF readers, security products) scanned for missing patches at least weekly?

Automatic updates alone do not count as checking. Something should report what is missing.

Are critical fixes for internet-facing software applied within 48 hours of release (or when a working exploit exists)?

Criticality is as rated by the vendor. Two days is the Maturity Level One target.

Are fixes for Office, browsers, email clients, PDF readers and security products applied within two weeks of release?

This is the everyday-software rule, and the one most small offices can meet with automatic updates plus a check.

Have you removed software that its maker no longer supports?

Unsupported software gets no security fixes at all, so the other answers stop mattering for it.

2

Patch operating systems

Keep Windows, macOS, servers and network gear (routers, firewalls) up to date.

Are all your computers, servers and network devices running an operating system the vendor still supports?

ASD requires unsupported operating systems to be replaced at Maturity Level One.

Are internet-facing servers and network devices (firewall, router) scanned for missing operating system patches at least daily?

If you have no servers and your router is managed by your internet provider, choose Does not apply.

Are workstations, internal servers and internal network devices scanned for missing operating system patches at least fortnightly?

Most small offices answer this through a managed device tool such as Intune or an IT provider's remote monitoring.

Are critical operating system fixes on internet-facing servers and network devices applied within 48 hours?

Non-critical fixes on those devices have a two-week limit.

Are operating system fixes on workstations, internal servers and internal network devices applied within one month of release?

One month is the Maturity Level One window for these devices.

3

Multi-factor authentication

A password alone is not enough: a second proof (an app code or security key) is needed to sign in.

Do staff use multi-factor authentication to sign in to your business's online services that hold sensitive data (email, cloud storage, accounting, CRM)?

Sensitive data means anything that would cause harm if it leaked: customer details, payroll, financials, contracts.

Is multi-factor authentication used on third-party services you use that hold sensitive data (internet banking, payroll, tax or accounting software, supplier portals)?

These are logins you hold with other companies, such as your bank or Xero.

Where a third-party service holding non-sensitive data offers multi-factor authentication, have you turned it on?

Social media, marketing tools and similar. The requirement says 'where available'.

If you run an online customer portal or account area with sensitive customer data, do customers (and your staff) have to use multi-factor authentication?

Most small businesses do not run one. If you do not, choose Does not apply.

Does your multi-factor method use two different kinds of proof (for example a password plus an authenticator app, or a security key unlocked by a PIN)?

Two passwords, or a password plus a security question, do not count. ASD asks for something you have plus something you know, or something you have unlocked by something you know or are.

4

Restrict administrative privileges

Few people should have the keys to everything, and those keys should not be used for reading email.

Does someone check and approve a request before a person is given administrator access to a system, application or data store?

Validated when first requested, not handed out by habit.

Does everyone with admin rights have a separate admin account that is used only for admin work?

Day-to-day work (email, browsing) happens on a normal account.

Are admin accounts blocked from email, web browsing and other internet services?

This stops a phishing email or a bad website from landing on an account that can change everything.

Are admin accounts that do need to use an online service (for example a cloud admin portal) limited to only what that duty requires?

Narrow permissions for the few admin accounts that must reach online services.

Are admin tasks done in a separate environment from everyday work, with everyday accounts unable to sign in to the admin environment?

For example admin tools run from a separate managed machine or session, and ordinary staff accounts cannot sign in to it.

5

Application control

Only approved programs can run on staff computers, so a downloaded nasty cannot simply start.

Is application control (for example Windows App Control for Business or AppLocker) turned on for your workstations?

It is separate from antivirus. Antivirus blocks known bad programs, application control blocks anything not on the approved list.

Does application control cover user profile folders and temporary folders used by the operating system, web browsers and email clients?

These are the places malware usually lands from a download or an email attachment.

Is the set of programs allowed to run (executables, libraries, scripts, installers, compiled HTML, HTML applications, control panel applets) limited to an approved list?

ASD names those file types explicitly at Maturity Level One.

6

Restrict Microsoft Office macros

Macros are small programs inside Word and Excel files. Attackers hide malicious ones in invoices and quotes.

Are Office macros switched off for everyone who has no proven business need for them?

Most small teams never use macros. If nobody does, disabling them for all is a full yes.

Are macros in files that come from the internet (downloads, email attachments) blocked?

Office has a setting for this, usually enforced through a policy so staff cannot click past it.

Is antivirus scanning of macros turned on?

This is a setting in Office's Trust Center and is on by default in current versions unless something has turned it off.

Can staff NOT change the macro security settings themselves?

Settings that a user can change are settings an attacker's document can talk a user into changing.

7

User application hardening

Switch off risky features in browsers and old software that attackers routinely abuse.

Is Internet Explorer 11 disabled or removed from all your computers?

It is a retired browser and Maturity Level One requires it gone.

Do your web browsers refuse to run Java from the internet?

Modern browsers do not, but older or customised setups can.

Do your web browsers block web advertisements from the internet?

Malicious adverts are a common delivery route. This is usually done with an ad-blocking extension set by policy.

Can staff NOT change the security settings in their web browsers?

Browser security settings should be set by policy and locked.

8

Regular backups

If ransomware, a flood or a mistake wipes your files, you can get them back, and an attacker could not wipe the backups too.

Are your data, applications and settings backed up and kept for as long as your business needs them?

Retention should match how critical each system is and your business continuity needs.

Are backups taken so you can restore everything to the same point in time?

Restoring your accounting file from Monday and your customer records from Friday can leave you with mismatched data.

Are backups stored securely and resiliently (for example a copy that is offline, immutable or in a separate account)?

A backup on a drive that is always plugged in is exactly what ransomware looks for.

Have you actually restored data, applications and settings from backup as part of a disaster recovery exercise?

A backup you have never restored is a hope, not a backup.

Can ordinary staff accounts NOT access backups that belong to other accounts?

Each person should reach only their own restore points.

Are ordinary staff accounts prevented from changing or deleting backups?

If a staff account is compromised, the backups should survive.

Your quick score

Answer the questions above and your score, strategy breakdown and gap list appear here. If you are unsure, answer No: an honest No is more useful than an optimistic Yes.

Source for every question: ASD Essential Eight maturity model, Appendix A (Maturity Level One). This is an estimate to help you plan. It is not an ASD assessment, an audit or a certification.

How the Essential Eight quick score works

Each answer earns points: Yes is 1, Partly is half a point and No is zero. A question marked Does not apply, such as a customer login portal you do not run, is left out of both your points and the total. The overall percentage is points earned divided by points available, and each of the eight strategies gets its own percentage so you can see where the weakness sits.

The gap list puts every No ahead of every Partly. Inside each group the order follows the eight strategies, which is a reading order, not a risk ranking. For where to begin when everything is a gap, see the suggested starting order below.

The questions cover these 8 strategies: patch applications, patch operating systems, multi-factor authentication, restrict administrative privileges, application control, restrict microsoft office macros, user application hardening, regular backups.

Essential Eight for small business: what Maturity Level One means

ASD defines three maturity levels. Maturity Level One is aimed at attackers who use commodity tools and opportunity: an unpatched online service, a stolen or reused password, a malicious document. It describes attackers who are looking for any victim, not your business in particular. That is the realistic threat for most small Australian businesses, and it is why Maturity Level One is the usual first target.

Reaching it is not only a technology job. Several requirements, such as removing unsupported software, keeping a restore tested and limiting who has admin rights, need a decision from the owner. Several others, such as application control and macro policy, are settings your IT provider applies once and checks on a schedule.

Does your small business need to meet the Essential Eight?

For most private businesses the Essential Eight is guidance rather than a legal rule. ASD recommends that all organisations implement it as a baseline, and Australian Government entities are expected to follow it under the Protective Security Policy Framework. Where it bites for a small business is the supply chain: a government agency, a larger customer or a cyber insurer may ask whether you meet it. Having a documented answer ready saves time and can save a contract.

The Essential Eight does not cover everything. It targets a specific set of common attacks. Privacy obligations, staff training and incident response are separate jobs, covered on our compliance page and in our guide to the Essential Eight for a one-van trade business.

Where to start if your score is low

This order is our judgement from the questions above, not an ASD ranking:

  • Multi-factor authentication on email and finance logins first. It is quick and removes the most common way in: a guessed or reused password.
  • Test a restore from backup. It tells you in an afternoon whether your backups work, and whether an attacker could delete them.
  • Remove unsupported software and operating systems. Nothing else protects a machine that no longer receives fixes.
  • Set update timeframes and get a report. Two weeks for everyday software, 48 hours for critical fixes on anything facing the internet.
  • Hand the settings strategies to your IT provider as one job: macros, browser hardening, application control and admin accounts.

Turn the score into evidence

A score is a starting point. A customer, insurer or tender will want proof: screenshots of policy settings, patch reports, a restore test record. Essential Eight self assessment: common questions

What is the Essential Eight?

The Essential Eight is a set of eight baseline cyber security mitigation strategies published by the Australian Signals Directorate (ASD) through the Australian Cyber Security Centre (ACSC): patch applications, patch operating systems, multi-factor authentication, restrict administrative privileges, application control, restrict Microsoft Office macros, user application hardening and regular backups.

Does a small business have to follow the Essential Eight?

For most private small businesses it is guidance, not a law. ASD recommends it as a baseline, and Australian Government entities are expected to follow it under the Protective Security Policy Framework. Larger customers, government buyers and some insurers may ask about it, so it is worth knowing where you stand before they do.

How long does this self assessment take?

Most people finish the 38 questions in 10 to 15 minutes. Some answers need your IT provider, so it works well as a shared checklist. Choose No when you do not know, because an unknown control cannot be counted as in place.

Is this score an official Essential Eight assessment?

No. It is an estimate based on ASD's Maturity Level One requirements, written in plain language. It does not test your systems, and it does not certify anything. A formal assessment follows ASD's assessment process and looks at real evidence.

What is the difference between Maturity Level One and higher levels?

Maturity Level One is aimed at opportunistic attackers using widely available tools. Levels Two and Three assume more capable and more selective attackers and ask for stricter timeframes, more logging and stronger multi-factor authentication. ASD describes each level in its maturity model.

Does my data get stored or sent anywhere?

No. The scoring runs in your browser with JavaScript. Your answers are not sent to us, saved or logged, and they disappear when you close or refresh the page.
